How to Get Teenagers to Do Chores (Without World War Three)

October 2026 · 6 min read · Parenting Tips

If you thought getting a seven-year-old to tidy their room was hard, wait until you try asking a fifteen-year-old to clean the bathroom. Teenagers and chores can feel like a recipe for daily battles — the sighing, the eye-rolls, the "in a minute" that stretches into never. Sound familiar?

The good news is that teenagers refusing chores isn't a sign of a failing family or a failed parent. It's developmental — and it absolutely can be turned around with the right approach. Not through more nagging, but through a smarter system.

With teenagers, the goal isn't compliance. It's buy-in. And the difference in how you get there is everything.

Why teenagers are different

The approach that works with a nine-year-old won't work with a sixteen-year-old, and understanding why is the first step. Teenagers are going through a developmental phase that is entirely designed to push back against parental authority. Their brains are literally wired to seek independence, assert identity, and test limits. When you tell a teenager to do something, their first instinct is often resistance — not laziness, but autonomy-seeking.

Add in a genuinely packed schedule — school, homework, extracurriculars, social life — and the resistance makes even more sense. The most effective strategies for teenagers work with this reality, not against it.

Have the conversation, not the command

With younger children, telling works reasonably well. With teenagers, it backfires. Instead of issuing instructions, have one clear conversation about household expectations — what needs doing, why it matters, and what's fair. Then write it down together.

This approach works for several reasons. First, it treats them like the almost-adults they are becoming. Second, it makes expectations explicit so there's no room for "I didn't know." Third, once it's agreed and written down, you don't need to keep asking — the agreement is the system.

The conversation might sound something like: "You're old enough now for us to run the house together properly. I need your help with a few things each week. What would feel fair to you?" Give them real input. You'll be surprised how reasonable most teenagers are when they're consulted rather than commanded.

Give them control over the when, not the whether

One of the most powerful adjustments parents can make with teenagers is shifting from "do this now" to "this needs to be done by X." Instead of telling your teenager to take the bins out as you're about to leave for work, establish that the bins go out by 7pm every Wednesday. Full stop. Whether they do it at 6pm or 6:55pm is entirely their call.

This preserves their sense of autonomy — they're managing their own time — while still getting the household need met. The rule exists; the execution is theirs. That distinction matters enormously at this age.

Teenagers don't resist chores because they're difficult. They resist being controlled. Give them the what and the when — but let them own the how and the now.

Connect to what they care about

The honest truth is that abstract reasoning — "we all have to pitch in" — lands differently with a fourteen-year-old than with a forty-year-old. Teenagers are motivated by very concrete things: going out with friends, screen time, Wi-Fi access, spending money. Connecting household contributions to those things isn't bribery — it's how the adult world actually works.

This doesn't have to mean paying for every chore (for a detailed look at how pocket money and chores can work together, see Should You Pay Kids for Chores? The Pocket Money Question Answered). But making clear that going out on Friday evening depends on having done your weekly chores is a completely reasonable household rule — and most teenagers respect it once it's consistently applied.

The key word is consistently. If the rule applies sometimes but not others, teenagers — who are excellent boundary-testers — will find every exception and exploit it.

What chores should teenagers actually be doing?

By the time a young person is 13 or 14, they are capable of far more than parents often ask. This is the age to start raising expectations meaningfully. By mid-teens, a young person can realistically take on:

  • Full ownership of their bedroom (including hoovering and changing their own bedding)
  • Cooking at least one full meal per week from scratch
  • Doing their own laundry — wash, dry, put away
  • Cleaning their bathroom if they have one to themselves
  • Taking responsibility for a recurring household task like bins, lawn, or food shopping
  • Washing up or loading/unloading the dishwasher regularly

The goal by 17 or 18 is a young person capable of running an independent household. That means cooking, cleaning, budgeting, and organising their own life. Gradually increasing expectations through the teenage years is how you get there — not leaving everything to the last year before they leave home. For a full breakdown of age-appropriate expectations from toddler upwards, see Age Appropriate Chores for Kids: The Complete Guide.

Stop nagging — use the system instead

Nagging is exhausting, ineffective, and tends to make teenagers dig in harder. Once you've had the conversation and agreed the expectations, your job is to hold the boundary rather than repeat the request. The difference sounds like this:

  • Nagging: "Have you done the bins yet? I asked you an hour ago. Why haven't you done them? You need to do them now."
  • Holding the boundary: "The bins need to be out by 7pm. It's 6:45." Then nothing more until either the bins are out or the consequence kicks in.

One short reminder is fine. The third reminder is where it breaks down. Once teenagers know that not doing a chore has a real consequence — and that the consequence reliably happens — most will comply without the daily battle you might be bracing for.

What if they still won't do it?

If a teenager consistently ignores agreed chore expectations despite a fair system and consistent consequences, it's worth having a different conversation entirely — not about the chores, but about what's going on for them. High stress around school, social difficulties, or low mood can all look like laziness from the outside. Approach it with curiosity before frustration.

And if it's just a bad week? Let it go. Pick your battles. A teenager who broadly contributes but has a rough patch is doing fine. Consistency over months matters more than perfection over days.
