Should You Pay Kids for Chores? The Pocket Money Question Answered

October 2026 · 6 min read · Parenting Tips

It's one of the most debated questions in parenting circles: should children earn pocket money for doing chores, or should helping out at home just be expected? Ask ten parents and you'll get ten different answers. Some swear by it. Others worry it teaches the wrong lesson. Most are muddling through somewhere in the middle.

The good news is that the research — and a lot of practical experience — points towards a clear approach. Not all-or-nothing, but a simple two-category system that teaches both responsibility and the value of earning. Here's how it works.

The key isn't whether you pay — it's what you pay for. Tying all chores to money can backfire badly. Tying some chores to money can work brilliantly.

Why paying for all chores can backfire

When every single chore comes with a price tag, a predictable problem emerges: your child starts treating the home like a workplace. They clock in, they do the job, they expect payment. The moment you can't pay — or forget, or decide it's not worth it for a small task — the chore simply doesn't happen. You've accidentally created a transactional relationship where "I only help if I'm paid."

There's also a subtle message embedded in paid-for-everything systems: that contributing to the household is optional, something you choose to do for reward rather than something you do because you live there. Most parents would rather their children grow up understanding that households run on shared effort, not individual transactions.

To make chores happen consistently in the first place — without the negotiation — it helps to remove friction entirely. Using a spin wheel to assign chores randomly means nobody can argue that they're being picked on, and adding a countdown timer turns the whole thing into a game. The two-category system that works

The most effective approach most families land on — often through trial and error — splits chores into two clear buckets:

  • Baseline chores — things everyone does because they live in the house. Tidying their own room, putting their dishes in the dishwasher, laying the table, sorting their school bag. These are not paid. They're just part of being a member of the household.
  • Bonus jobs — tasks that go beyond the basics. Vacuuming the stairs, washing the car, weeding the garden, cleaning the bathroom. These are optional, available to earn, and come with a small payment attached.

This split works because it maintains the principle that everyone contributes for free, while also giving children a genuine way to earn when they want something. When your child says "I want that new game," your answer can be "here are three jobs that would earn you the money for it" — and suddenly motivation appears from nowhere.

How much should bonus jobs pay?

There's no universal right answer here, but a commonly used UK guideline is to make the payment feel proportional to both the effort and the child's age. A rough starting point:

  • Ages 5–7: 20–30p for a simple bonus job (wiping surfaces, sorting recycling)
  • Ages 8–11: 50p–£1 for a medium job (vacuuming a room, washing up all the dishes)
  • Ages 12+: £1–£3 for a bigger job (mowing the lawn, cleaning the bathroom from top to bottom)

The amounts matter less than the consistency. Agree the rate in advance, write it down somewhere visible, and stick to it. Renegotiating every time creates resentment and confusion.

What about baseline pocket money?

Many families run a baseline pocket money amount — a fixed weekly sum that isn't tied to any chore at all. This teaches children to manage money regardless of whether they've had a productive week. Then bonus jobs sit on top of this, giving them the ability to earn more when motivated.

A popular rule of thumb in the UK is roughly 50p to £1 per year of age per week as a baseline — so a 9-year-old might receive £4.50 to £9 per week. But frankly, whatever your family can afford and feels fair is the right answer. The structure matters more than the specific figure.

Consistent small amounts beat inconsistent large amounts every time. Children learn to manage money by having money to manage — regularly, predictably, and without strings attached.

Making the chore side of it actually work

Even the best pocket money system won't help if the chores themselves are a daily battle. The same principles that make unpaid chores work apply to paid ones too — clear expectations, a defined end point, and an element of fun.

For baseline chores especially, keeping things light matters. A spin wheel to decide who does what means nobody can claim they always get the worst jobs — it's fair because it's random. A countdown timer turns a potentially tedious ten minutes into a race. See How to Get Kids to Do Chores Without Arguing for a full breakdown of strategies that make the daily routine smoother.

For bonus jobs, older children especially respond well to a clear "menu" — a list on the fridge or whiteboard showing which jobs are available, how long they take, and how much they pay. Giving them the autonomy to choose which ones they take on builds a sense of agency that plain chore lists never achieve.

Getting the ages right

One thing that trips up many families is assigning chores — paid or unpaid — that simply aren't appropriate for the child's age or ability. A seven-year-old can absolutely set the table and tidy their room, but asking them to clean the oven is setting everyone up to fail. For a comprehensive breakdown of what children can realistically handle at each stage, see Age Appropriate Chores for Kids: The Complete Guide.

Getting the match right matters for the pocket money system too. A job that's too hard leads to a poor result and an argument about whether it was done properly. A job that's too easy feels like a trick. Aim for the sweet spot: something that takes genuine effort but is clearly achievable.

A simple system to start today

If you want to get going this week, here's a starting point that many UK families find works well:

  1. Decide on 2–3 baseline chores per child that are expected every day, no payment
  2. Write a list of 4–5 bonus jobs with agreed prices per job
  3. Set a small weekly baseline pocket money amount
  4. Use a spin wheel for baseline chores to keep fairness simple
  5. Let children pick from the bonus job list whenever they want to earn extra
